Cabinet door guide

10 Cabinet Door Types Explained

Compare melamine, UV, PET, lacquer, PVC membrane, wood veneer, leather, aluminium-frame glass, solid wood and stainless steel doors by construction, performance, appearance and project fit.

Updated August 25, 2026  ·  15-minute read

Cabinet doors define the visible style of a kitchen or wardrobe, but appearance is only one part of the decision. The substrate, surface finish, edge treatment and door construction also influence durability, cleaning, colour consistency, moisture resistance, production time and price.

Names can be confusing because some terms describe the core and others describe only the finish. For example, PET, UV and lacquer doors commonly use MDF, while a melamine door may use particleboard, MDF or plywood.

This guide compares ten widely used cabinet door options and explains where each one performs best.

First understand the complete door

  1. Core: particleboard, MDF, plywood, OSB, glass or another structural material.
  2. Face finish: melamine paper, UV coating, PET film, lacquer, PVC membrane, veneer, leather or glass.
  3. Edge system: ABS/PVC edge banding, continuous membrane wrapping, painted edges or aluminium framing.
  4. Profile: flat slab, routed design, shaker profile, raised panel or framed glass.

Specify the whole system. A finish name alone is not enough. Confirm the core, thickness, front and back finish, edge material, adhesive, colour, sheen, handle machining and maximum door size.

1. Melamine doors: practical, consistent and cost-effective

Melamine doors are produced by pressing resin-impregnated decorative paper onto a wood-based core. Particleboard is common for standard flat doors, while MDF or plywood may be selected when the project requires a different weight, fixing performance or moisture specification.

The surface can reproduce solid colours, textiles, stone patterns and realistic wood grains. Because the face is factory-finished, production is efficient and colour matching is generally consistent. The main quality checkpoints are the substrate, surface grade, edge banding, adhesive line and corner finishing.

2. UV-coated doors: polished high gloss for modern interiors

UV doors use a coating that is cured under ultraviolet light to form a hard, smooth decorative surface. MDF is commonly used because its flat, uniform face supports a consistent high-gloss finish.

These doors suit clean contemporary designs and are easy to wipe, but the reflective surface can reveal fingerprints, dust, scratches and uneven substrate preparation. Buyers should inspect gloss level, colour uniformity, protective film, edge match and the reverse-side finish.

Best forContemporary glossy kitchens, wall units and feature panels

3. PET doors: refined matte surfaces for handleless designs

PET doors use a polyester-based decorative film laminated to a smooth panel, most often MDF. The finish may be matte, soft-touch or high gloss, although super-matte anti-fingerprint surfaces are especially popular in modern kitchens.

PET gives a consistent colour and pleasant tactile finish. Door performance depends on the quality of the film, lamination, adhesive and edge banding. A good sample review should include fingerprint visibility, scratch behaviour, edge colour and resistance to household cleaning products.

Best forHandleless kitchens, minimalist wardrobes and contemporary flat-panel furniture
Available looksSuper-matte, soft-touch, satin, gloss and solid colours

4. Lacquered doors: custom colour and flexible profiling

Lacquered doors are normally made from MDF that is sanded, primed, coated and cured through multiple finishing stages. They can be produced as flat slabs, shaker doors, routed panels or integrated-handle profiles.

The continuous coating creates seamless faces and edges and allows broad colour control. Quality varies with substrate preparation, coating system, film build, curing and polishing. Lacquer is premium rather than indestructible: sharp impacts can chip edges and dark high-gloss colours show marks more easily.

5. PVC membrane doors: shaped profiles without separate edge banding

PVC membrane doors, also called thermofoil or vacuum-formed doors, use a heated film drawn over a routed MDF face under vacuum. The film wraps the front and edges in one continuous layer, making it suitable for shaker and raised-panel designs.

This method delivers many styles at a competitive price, but film, adhesive, temperature control and routing geometry must work together. Heat near ovens, steam appliances or direct sunlight can stress low-quality films or bonding, so installation clearances and heat shields matter.

6. Wood veneer doors: authentic grain with an engineered core

Wood veneer doors use thin slices of real timber bonded to a stable panel such as plywood or MDF. The construction preserves natural grain while reducing the movement and material cost associated with wide solid-wood panels.

Every veneer sheet is naturally different, so grain selection, book matching, sequence matching, stain and clear coating have a major effect on the result. Samples should represent the approved species, cut, colour range and finish—not only a generic wood name.

7. Leather-faced doors: tactile detail for premium wardrobes

Leather-faced doors use natural leather, microfibre leather or another engineered leather surface over a stable board, often combined with an aluminium frame. They are selected primarily for visual and tactile impact rather than for standard kitchen volume.

The surface can soften a wardrobe or dressing-room design and coordinate with upholstered interiors. Buyers should verify the exact leather type, abrasion resistance, colourfastness, cleanability, seam or corner treatment and frame finish.

8. Aluminium-frame glass doors: light, modern and display-focused

These doors combine clear, fluted, tinted or opaque glass with a slim aluminium frame. They introduce transparency and contrast without relying on a wood-based face panel, making them useful for display cabinets and feature zones.

Safety and hardware selection are critical. Specify the glass type and thickness, edge treatment, safety film or tempering, frame finish, corner joint, hinge compatibility and maximum door size. Smoked or textured glass can reduce visual clutter compared with fully clear glass.

9. Solid wood doors: authentic timber and traditional joinery

Solid wood doors are normally constructed from timber rails and stiles surrounding a centre panel. Oak, ash, maple, walnut and other species create genuine grain, depth and colour variation that printed surfaces cannot fully reproduce. Depending on the design, the centre may be solid timber or an engineered panel selected to improve dimensional stability.

Timber expands and contracts as humidity changes, so kiln drying, moisture content, grain selection, joint design and coating quality all matter. A well-made door can be sanded, repaired or refinished, but natural variation must be accepted and approved through representative samples. Buyers should also distinguish true solid-wood construction from a veneer-faced board.

10. Stainless steel doors: durable performance for wet and high-use areas

Stainless steel cabinet doors usually use a thin metal facing folded over a stable core, although formed or framed constructions are also available. The non-porous surface is easy to wipe and suits wet zones, high-use kitchens and contemporary interiors where durability and a precise industrial appearance are priorities.

Not every stainless-steel door performs the same way. Confirm the alloy grade, sheet thickness, core material, edge construction, brushing direction and surface protection. Grade 304 is widely specified for many indoor applications, but the correct grade still depends on moisture, salt exposure, cleaning chemicals and the project environment. Satin surfaces reduce glare, yet fingerprints, fine scratches and dents may remain visible.

Selection recommendations

  • For cost-effective volume kitchens: choose melamine doors with verified core and precise edge banding.
  • For modern high gloss: choose UV when a flat reflective surface and efficient production are priorities.
  • For super-matte handleless designs: PET offers a consistent modern finish; approve the exact film and edge sample.
  • For custom colours or routed doors: lacquer provides the greatest design flexibility but needs a controlled finishing process.
  • For affordable shaker and raised profiles: PVC membrane is practical when film, adhesive and heat clearances are properly specified.
  • For authentic natural grain with a stable panel: use wood veneer and define the species, cut, matching and stain range.
  • For luxury wardrobes: leather-faced doors work best as selected feature elements rather than every door.
  • For display and visual contrast: use aluminium-frame glass with the correct safety glass and compatible hinges.
  • For authentic traditional or premium timber: choose solid wood and verify the species, moisture content, joints and centre-panel construction.
  • For wet, high-use or commercial-inspired spaces: consider stainless steel and confirm the grade, core, sheet thickness and edge fabrication.

Always approve a complete door sample. Review the face, edge, reverse, corner, colour, sheen and handle machining under the lighting expected in the finished project.
